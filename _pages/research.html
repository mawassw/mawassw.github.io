My research runs across two connected programs organized around a shared question: how much does a population’s history determine what it can do next — and can we know that before it matters?

Program I — Evolutionary Genetics of Adaptation and Persistence

For readers from evolutionary biology, ecology, integrative biology, and conservation genetics.

This program studies what constrains evolutionary response in wild and historical populations: whether a population can adapt and decline simultaneously; how much of its heritable variation selection can actually use; and what a population crash costs in the direction of remaining variation. It draws on long-term pedigree records from monitored vertebrate populations and preindustrial human genealogies, forward-time simulation across finite genomes, and analytical quantitative genetics. Most directly relevant threads: Mutational drought and the fate of small populations, Evolutionary predictability in human populations, Linked selection and the neutral diversity we actually observe.

Program II — Population History and Human Genetic Architecture

For readers from genetics, human genetics, genomics, and molecular and cell biology.

This program asks what population history did to the variation underlying complex traits that medicine now predicts from: how much sits in rare variants selection held at low frequency; whether ancestry stratification that survives PCA correction propagates coherently into polygenic scores and selection tests; and whether a trait’s genetic basis turns over between populations fast enough that a score cannot follow it. It draws on random matrix theory, allele frequency spectra, and biobank-scale GWAS data. Most directly relevant thread: Population stratification and the limits of polygenic inference. The quantitative-genetic and linked-selection work in Program I provides the analytical foundation that connects both programs — population history acting on variation twice, in wild populations and in clinical prediction alike.
