My research addresses three questions about how genetic variation is generated, maintained, and measured, and what it determines about evolutionary outcomes. They span wild vertebrate populations, historical human populations, and contemporary biobanks, because the same problem recurs in each: central quantities in evolutionary and human genetics, such as additive genetic variance, effective population size, and ancestry, are not observed directly but inferred through models whose assumptions need to be tested.

When does heritable variation predict evolutionary change?

Classical quantitative genetics predicts the response to selection from a trait’s additive genetic variance and the strength of selection acting on it. These predictions inform conservation, agriculture, and forecasts of how populations respond to environmental change. Natural populations routinely depart from them: some exhibit evolutionary stasis despite abundant heritable variation and consistent directional selection, while others undergo rapid phenotypic change that still ends in extinction. Heritability and additive genetic variance compress the genetic basis of a trait into a single number, and miss the details that govern long-term evolutionary response: rare alleles, transient linkage disequilibrium, and pleiotropic correlations among traits. Because these details are shaped by population history, a population’s past constrains its future evolutionary capacity.

I proposed mutational drought as a mechanism of genetically driven extinction distinct from mutational meltdown: as a population declines, its supply of beneficial mutations shrinks, leaving too few to compensate for deleterious mutations or to track a changing environment. Perturbation analysis and whole-genome forward-time simulations show that, near the critical population size, this shortage is more dangerous than the accumulation of deleterious mutations. In the French-Canadian genealogies, I showed that genotype-by-environment interactions reshape the additive genetic variance of reproductive timing and its covariance with fitness, and that three sister populations sharing the same ancestry exhibited evolutionary stasis despite heritability and consistent directional selection. I am now developing theory for the cost of evolutionary rescue: rapid adaptation in a declining population relies on a few highly pleiotropic mutations, which can leave its genetic covariances misaligned with the direction of future selection.

What does an estimated genetic effect measure?

Genome-wide association studies have shown that complex traits are extensively polygenic, and their effect size estimates are used in polygenic prediction, heritability estimation, and tests for natural selection in humans. These analyses typically treat an allele’s effect as a fixed, context-independent parameter. In reality, an estimated effect is jointly shaped by ancestral background, linkage disequilibrium, assortative mating, the familial environment, and demographic history. Separating direct causal effects from demographic and genealogical confounding is necessary to interpret genetic associations biologically, and to distinguish genuine signals of selection from artifacts of population structure.

Using random matrix theory, I showed that principal components estimated from finite samples can be statistically significant while still too imprecise to remove the ancestry differences they detect. Residual bias is greatest where population structure first becomes detectable, and it propagates into polygenic scores and tests for polygenic selection. Building on this framework, I am developing theory to predict the attenuation of within-family relative to population-level effect sizes from assortative mating and population structure; to partition gene-by-environment interaction from environmentally responsive genetic nurture, using transmitted and non-transmitted parental alleles; and to determine whether the decline of genetic effect sizes with age reflects antagonistic pleiotropy or survivorship bias.

What generates and maintains genetic variation?

Additive genetic variance in fitness is the substrate of adaptive change. Because heritable differences in reproductive success reduce effective population size, it also reduces neutral diversity across the genome. How much fitness variance natural populations carry, what maintains it, and how much neutral diversity it removes are long-standing questions in evolutionary genetics. Their answers bear on nearly every inference based on neutral diversity, from effective population size and demographic history to the genetic assessment of threatened populations. The same processes of mutation, selection, and drift also determine the genetic architecture of complex traits and diseases, including why so many loci contribute to them.

Comparing classical theory with published pedigree estimates from 19 wild vertebrate populations, I found that measured fitness variance is too small to explain the observed deficits in neutral diversity, yet too large to be maintained under mutation–selection–drift balance. Adjusting the estimates in either direction closes one gap but widens the other, indicating that these populations are far from equilibrium. I also study how selection on disease liability shapes the frequency and ancestral state of risk alleles, the effect of stabilizing selection on linked neutral diversity, and how polygenicity can arise without positive selection.

Approach and expertise

I combine analytical theory with simulation and empirical data. I derive the values that unobserved quantities should take under explicit models of mutation, selection, drift, and demography, test those predictions with forward-time simulations, and compare them with estimates from pedigreed populations and genomic data.

  • Analytical theory. Quantitative and population genetics, diffusion and moment equations, site frequency spectra, eco-evolutionary dynamics, and moving-optimum models of multivariate adaptation, including Fisher’s geometric model.
  • Random matrix theory. Principal component correction for population structure in biobank-scale data, and the structure of multivariate genetic variation (the G-matrix).
  • Simulation. Forward-time whole-genome simulators written in C, SLiM, msprime, and tskit, run on high-performance computing clusters.
  • Statistical quantitative genetics. Animal models and Bayesian mixed models for partitioning genetic and environmental variance in pedigreed populations, and sensitivity analyses of pedigree attributes.
  • Data. Multigenerational French-Canadian genealogies spanning millions of records, long-term pedigree studies of wild vertebrates, UK Biobank and HGDP genotypes, and GWAS summary statistics.
